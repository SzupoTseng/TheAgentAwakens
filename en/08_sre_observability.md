# Chapter 8 · Observability: Don't Let Sand Get in Your Eyes

## Scenario 31 · Dynamic Pruning of Prometheus Metric High Cardinality Explosions
**Background**: A federated Prometheus cluster spanning 1,800 microservices ingests roughly 24 million active series per second, with the TSDB hovering at the edge of memory.
**Problem**: One team stuffed `user_id` and `request_uuid` into metric labels. Within three hours, active series spiked from 24 million to 190 million, the Prometheus head block consumed 220GB of RAM, and after being OOMKilled it took 17 minutes to replay the WAL on restart — during those 17 minutes, every alerting rule across the entire organization was blind, and not a single dashboard would light up.
**Skills Required**: PromQL cardinality analysis, understanding of TSDB internals, relabel_config design, capacity planning.
**Open-Source Tools**: Prometheus, `promtool`, Grafana Mimir, cardinality-exporter.
**Agent Capabilities Used**: The Lobster (OpenClaw) subscribes to the second derivative of `prometheus_tsdb_head_series`; when the slope goes anomalous it automatically runs `promtool tsdb analyze` and queries the TSDB `/api/v1/status/tsdb`. A sub-agent localizes the explosion to the `request_uuid` label on `payment-gw` within 30 seconds, generates a PR with `metric_relabel_configs` drop rules locally, and via the Multi-Channel Gateway simultaneously posts the "top three labels about to trip the breaker" to Slack and the on-call channel, complete with a heatmap.
**Without the Agent**: A human first has to notice RAM climbing, then manually crawl `/api/v1/status/tsdb` to dig through the top-10 series, eyeball-compare labels, write a relabel rule, and wait for a config reload — by which point the explosion has usually already OOMed before the investigation even begins.
**Payoff**: Detection-to-pruning-PR for runaway cardinality dropped from an average of 25 minutes to 40 seconds; quarterly TSDB OOM incidents fell from 4 to 0.

> 💡 A Word to the Wise
> "A cardinality explosion isn't a disk problem — it's a cognitive problem of 'someone treating infinite dimensions as free.' A metric's value lies in its being aggregatable; a dimension that can't be aggregated is just a bill disguised as observability."

> 🔍 Advanced Commentary — Where the Road Leads
> This case pushes the Lobster down the road of "metric governance gatekeeper": not just pruning after the explosion, but intercepting high-cardinality label commits at the CI stage. The engineering hurdle in landing this is the trust boundary — automatically dropping a label is equivalent to deciding on another team's behalf that "this dimension isn't worth observing," and if you drop a critical dimension by mistake, you've created a new blind spot instead. What the Lobster will need next is "reversible, negotiated pruning": downsample first, then flag, then give the owner a confirmation window — rather than cutting things off outright.

## Scenario 32 · Blind-Test Root-Cause Tracing of APM Performance Regression Across Microservices
**Background**: A 14-hop critical checkout path spanning 6 teams and 3 languages, with APM built on OpenTelemetry + Tempo.
**Problem**: After a release, P99 latency drifted from 180ms to 540ms, yet every team's view of their own service's dashboard was "green" — because the regression was spread evenly across every hop, and no single service exceeded its own SLO threshold. This is the classic gray failure: the total blew up, but nobody is the culprit.
**Skills Required**: Distributed tracing analysis, statistical diff profiling, cross-service baseline construction, Apdex interpretation.
**Open-Source Tools**: OpenTelemetry, Grafana Tempo, Jaeger, Pyroscope.
**Agent Capabilities Used**: The Lobster runs span-level quantile diffs over "10,000 traces each from before and after the release." A sub-agent discovers the regression isn't in any single span, but in an `auth-sidecar` interceptor newly added to every hop — an mTLS certificate verification changed from cached to a synchronous RPC, adding +25ms per hop. The Lobster runs a Pyroscope flame-graph diff locally to corroborate that CPU didn't rise — wait time did — then @-mentions the platform team that changed the sidecar in the channel, attaching the cumulative evidence of "14 hops × 25ms."
**Without the Agent**: Six teams trade blame on the incident bridge for three hours, each able to prove "my service didn't regress," and nobody looks at the sidecar that cuts across all of them.
**Payoff**: Cross-team finger-pointing time converged from an average of 3 hours to 8 minutes; regression localization precision down to a single commit.

> 💡 A Word to the Wise
> "When everyone's dashboard is green yet the system is slowing down, the problem is always hiding in 'the layer nobody owns' — a cross-cutting concern is the physical developing-print of an organizational blind spot."

> 🔍 Advanced Commentary — Where the Road Leads
> This points to the Lobster's road as "full-chain statistician": upgrading root-cause analysis from "find the service over its limit" to "find the distribution over its limit." The forward-looking engineering challenge is causation rather than correlation — a span diff can tell you where things got slow, but it can't prove that thing caused it. The next step is for the Lobster to tap into change event streams (deploy/flag/config) and align the inflection points of the latency distribution against changes on the timeline, so it can move from "correlational accusation" to "trustworthy causal attribution."

## Scenario 33 · Dynamic Online Downgrade of Runaway Log Levels
**Background**: A production cluster of 600 Pods using standard logback/zap, with logs shipped through Fluent Bit into Loki.
**Problem**: During one troubleshooting session, someone temporarily bumped a hot-path service's log level from INFO to DEBUG and forgot to revert it. At QPS peak, that service spat out 900,000 log lines per second, the Loki ingester got slammed to 429, and log queries company-wide began timing out — worse still, the genuinely useful ERRORs were drowned in a sea of DEBUG and couldn't be searched at all.
**Skills Required**: Dynamic configuration of logging frameworks (actuator/logging endpoint), Loki rate-limit tuning, log-volume attribution, hot-path identification.
**Open-Source Tools**: Loki, Fluent Bit, Spring Boot Actuator, Vector.
**Agent Capabilities Used**: The Lobster watches for a step change in Loki's `distributor_bytes_received_total`. A sub-agent uses LogQL to attribute the volume surge to a single logger on a single service within 90 seconds, calls that service's actuator `/loggers` endpoint to bring the logger back down to INFO online (no restart), and via the Multi-Channel Gateway reports "whose level I temporarily lowered, why, and when it auto-restores," along with the audit record of who changed it to DEBUG at 14:02.
**Without the Agent**: The on-call scrambles to find which service is spewing, and once found still has to coordinate with the owner to revert the setting and go through a release process — meanwhile the logging system has long since been overwhelmed, to the point that even the log of "who changed the level" can't be queried.
**Payoff**: Stanching a log storm dropped from an average of 22 minutes to 90 seconds; ERROR searchability recovered from 12% during the storm back to 99%.

> 💡 A Word to the Wise
> "DEBUG is the searchlight of troubleshooting, but a searchlight left on illuminates the very runway you're crashing on — the opposite of observability isn't no logs, it's so many logs there's no signal."

> 🔍 Advanced Commentary — Where the Road Leads
> This carries the Lobster down the road of "warden of temporary changes": every debugging online change should carry a TTL, and what the Lobster does is backstop human forgetfulness. The trust boundary in landing this is subtle — if the Lobster reverts someone's DEBUG mid-troubleshooting, it may cut off exactly the lead they were still examining. The future solution isn't an outright downgrade but a "negotiated TTL": when a dangerous level change is detected, flag it and count down first, give the original operator the option to keep or renew it, and trigger override-and-stanch only as the system approaches a breaker trip.

## Scenario 34 · Real-Time Cleanup When Runaway Container Logs Crash the Host Disk
**Background**: Bare-metal Kubernetes nodes, with container logs written to the host's `/var/lib/docker/containers` and no log-rotation cap configured.
**Problem**: A Pod stuck in a CrashLoopBackOff spewed stacktraces to stderr every second; a single container wrote 480GB in 8 hours and filled the host root partition to 100% — the kubelet lost the ability to write, the node went NotReady, the 30 innocent Pods on it were evicted by association, and the avalanche began spreading to neighboring nodes.
**Skills Required**: Linux filesystem and inode diagnostics, kubelet/containerd mechanics, disk-pressure eviction policy, safe log truncation.
**Open-Source Tools**: containerd, `logrotate`, node-problem-detector, ncdu.
**Agent Capabilities Used**: The Lobster's node-exporter sentinel triggers when `node_filesystem_avail_bytes` drops below 5%. A sub-agent logs into the node, runs `du`/`ncdu` to pin down the offending container's log file within 20 seconds, and a local script safely empties it with `truncate -s 0` (rather than `rm`, to avoid an fd leak that holds the space without releasing it), while cordoning the Pod to stop it from migrating to another node and continuing its rampage, and reports to the channel "which file I cleared, how much I freed, and why I chose truncate."
**Without the Agent**: The on-call is woken at 3 a.m. by PagerDuty, SSHes in, sees 100% on `df -h`, and in a panic `rm`s the log file that's still being written to — the space doesn't get freed at all (the fd still holds it), the node keeps dying, and the avalanche expands to three nodes.
**Payoff**: Stanching a disk-full incident dropped from an average of 18 minutes (including the human finding the correct remediation) to 35 seconds; Pods evicted by association fell from an average of 30+ to 0.

> 💡 A Word to the Wise
> "At 3 a.m. deleting logs, the difference between `rm` and `truncate` is the difference between 'the node revives' and 'the node plays dead' — an SRE's muscle memory is bought one avalanche at a time."

> 🔍 Advanced Commentary — Where the Road Leads
> This road is "the reflex arc at the edge of a resource cliff": what the Lobster does is precisely the class of operation a human, sleep-deprived, is most likely to get wrong. The forward-looking risk is the "collateral damage" of automated remediation — the log it truncates may be the sole evidence for the post-incident RCA. What the Lobster should do in the future isn't simply emptying, but "sample-and-archive before truncating": before clearing, dump the last N tens of thousands of lines to object storage, so stanching and evidence preservation are no longer either/or — and that is the precondition for anyone daring to hand root to an Agent.

## Scenario 35 · Ending Cross-Team Finger-Pointing with Distributed Tracing Flame Graphs
**Background**: A 22-hop search request chain whose flame graph spans 7 teams; every major incident requires pulling a crowd onto a "war-room bridge."
**Problem**: Search P99 occasionally spikes to 3 seconds. On the incident bridge, 18 people talk past each other; the flame graph is too wide and too deep for anyone to grasp the whole picture; the echo of "not me" reverberates through the conference room for 90 minutes; and the span that actually consumed 2.4 seconds gets ignored by everyone because it's named `internal_call_42`.
**Skills Required**: Reading the critical path of a flame graph, span ownership identification, tail-latency sampling analysis, maintaining a cross-team responsibility map.
**Open-Source Tools**: Jaeger, Grafana Tempo, Parca, speedscope.
**Agent Capabilities Used**: The Lobster automatically computes the critical path for slow traces (not the widest span, but the chain that actually gates total duration). A sub-agent resolves `internal_call_42` back through the service ownership map to "the indexing team's cache-warming lock," computes that it accounts for 2.4s / 3.0s = 80% of the responsibility, then directly @-mentions that team's owner in the channel with a stripped-down flame graph that removes all irrelevant spans and keeps only the critical path. Before the incident bridge is even fully staffed, the culprit has already been named.
**Without the Agent**: 18 people take turns proving their innocence on the bridge, the flame graph scrolls back and forth on the shared screen with nobody able to read it, and half the MTTR is eaten by the act of "finding who's responsible" itself.
**Payoff**: Cross-team attribution dropped from an average of 90 minutes to 5 minutes; the average headcount on the incident bridge fell from 18 to 4 (only the truly relevant people get pulled in).

> 💡 A Word to the Wise
> "The most dangerous span in a flame graph is often the one with the most boring name — something called `internal_call_42` is exactly that precisely because nobody was willing to name it, so nobody is willing to own it."

> 🔍 Advanced Commentary — Where the Road Leads
> This points to the Lobster's most politically lethal road: "automatic attributor." The technical core of ending finger-pointing is turning the flame graph from a "visual" into a "responsibility vector along the critical path." But the trust boundary here is extremely sensitive — when an Agent names a team as the culprit in the channel, it must bear the cost of being refuted. For this road to work, the Lobster's attribution must come with a "replayable evidence bundle" (trace id + span + ownership source) and a confidence interval on its own attribution; naming names is power, but only auditable naming becomes an institution.

## Scenario 36 · Alert Storm Deduplication and Root-Cause Folding
**Background**: A monitoring system with 4,000 alerting rules, with Alertmanager wired to PagerDuty and several on-call channels.
**Problem**: The core database primary failed, cascading into 312 alerts flooding the on-call channel within 90 seconds — connection pool full, upstream timeouts, retry storms, cache penetration, disk IO spiking… the on-call's phone buzzed itself hot, and the real root cause, "the DB primary is down," was buried in the 187th notification, unseen. This is a gray failure manufactured by the alerting system itself.
**Skills Required**: Alert topology modeling, causal correlation/folding, inhibition rule design, on-call cognitive-load management.
**Open-Source Tools**: Alertmanager, Karma, Keep, Grafana OnCall.
**Agent Capabilities Used**: Within the 90-second window, the Lobster folds the 312 alerts causally according to the service dependency graph. A sub-agent determines that 311 of them are downstream symptoms of "DB primary failure," escalates only the 1 root-cause alert to a P1 push, and folds the other 311 into an expandable appendix. The Multi-Channel Gateway posts "root cause + affected downstream list + suggested failover commands" all at once, and automatically acks the 311 noise alerts.
**Without the Agent**: The on-call swipes frantically through 312 notifications hunting for clues, manually acking until their thumb cramps, and by the time they finally piece together "it's the DB that's down," the golden window for a graceful failover has long since closed.
**Payoff**: Alert-noise compression ratio of 312:1; storm-start-to-root-cause dropped from an average of 14 minutes to 50 seconds.

> 💡 A Word to the Wise
> "312 alerts aren't 312 problems, they're 312 cries from 1 problem — the on-call's pain was never the absence of alerts, but the alerting system retelling 'one heartbreak' three hundred times."

> 🔍 Advanced Commentary — Where the Road Leads
> This road pushes the Lobster toward being an "editor of alerts" rather than a "mover of alerts." Folding is fundamentally cognitive offloading for the on-call, conditioned on the dependency topology being accurate enough — once the topology goes stale, the Lobster may mis-fold a genuinely independent alert into someone else's story and silence it. The engineering crux ahead is "self-learning topology": inferring service dependencies from historical alert co-occurrence, and retaining a "mis-fold risk score" for each folding decision — when risk is high, fold less rather than kill by mistake. The red line of observability automation is always: better noisy than missing.

## Scenario 37 · Clock Skew Scrambling Traces — Authoritative Arbitration of "Which Clock to Trust"
**Background**: 1,400 services across three AZs and roughly 6,000 nodes, with distributed tracing relying on each node's local clock to timestamp spans. NTP is inherently **probabilistic synchronization**: under normal conditions, inter-node residuals already carry 1–20ms of jitter — that's baseline noise, not a fault.
**Problem**: One AZ's upstream stratum-2 time source silently went unreachable, chrony entered holdover and free-ran on the local crystal oscillator, the clock drifted slowly at about 35 ppm, and over eight hours accumulated to −400ms. The flame graphs began showing negative latency where "a child span finishes before its parent." The difficulty is: (1) the drift is **continuous and monotonic**, not a clean step, so "jump detection" can't catch it; (2) the three AZs drift independently, in different directions and magnitudes, with no global ground truth; (3) `node_timex_offset_seconds` itself is collected by node_exporter at 15s intervals and then enters storage via remote_write — **the collection pipeline itself has second-level latency and timestamps**, and using one untrustworthy clock to measure another untrustworthy clock is a circular argument.
**Skills Required**: NTP/chrony holdover mechanics, the semantics of each `node_timex` field, multi-source fault-tolerant arbitration (Marzullo/Chrony falseticker elimination), relative measurement of cross-AZ offset, PTP cross-reference.
**Open-Source Tools**: chrony (`chronyc tracking` / `sources -v` / `sourcestats`), Prometheus node-exporter (`node_timex_offset_seconds` / `node_timex_maxerror_seconds` / `node_ntp_root_dispersion`), Jaeger, OpenTelemetry, (as reference baseline) PTP `phc2sys`.
**Agent Capabilities Used**: The Lobster first catches the non-physical signature — a batch of traces shows negative duration and inverted causality. The sub-agent **does not directly trust any single node's offset number**; instead it arbitrates over time sources: (1) for each AZ it takes a set of nodes and reads chrony's health signals — has `Leap status` flipped to `Not synchronized`, did stratum jump from 2 to 16, did `Root dispersion` surge from <5ms to >200ms, did the `sourcestats` skew (ppm) estimate error exceed 10 ppm — these are the smoking gun of holdover, more trustworthy than raw offset; (2) it performs **pairwise relative offset measurement** across the three AZs (using the server/client timestamp difference of the same batch of simultaneously arriving spans, rather than absolute clocks), finding that AZ-B is consistently −400ms off relative to the other two and that the gap widens monotonically, while AZ-A/C agree with each other — using "majority consensus" to judge AZ-B the falseticker; (3) it cross-confirms that AZ-B's entire chrony `root dispersion` surged in lockstep and the upstream source's reach dropped to 0, confirming a time-source disconnect rather than an individual-node issue. Conclusion within 60 seconds: "this isn't network jitter, AZ-B's clock is drifting in holdover and it's the minority — trust the other two AZs," attaching the offset-node list and relative-offset curve, and recommending **compensating AZ-B's traces for offset on the analysis side first** (rather than crudely restarting chrony to force a step — that would tear the timestamps of in-flight spans once more).
**Without the Agent**: The team mistakes the continuous drift for network jitter or a code bug and debugs in the wrong direction for an entire day; even if someone thinks to check the clocks, they get stuck on "which AZ's offset to actually trust" — because there's no global ground truth, all three are moving, and the eye simply can't arbitrate which is the falseticker.
**Payoff**: Converged "a full day chasing phantom jitter" to a 1-minute root-cause localization, and the conclusion it delivers is arbitrable — "which AZ, drifted how much, why it's untrustworthy" — rather than a single-point number; avoided an emergency release built on a false premise.

> 💡 A Word to the Wise
> "When the flame graph tells you 'the effect happened before the cause,' don't doubt causality — doubt the clock first. But the harder part isn't discovering the clock is wrong; it's answering 'which one to trust' among three clocks all secretly drifting. A distributed system has no god's-eye true time; all you can do is let the majority vote the minority out."

> 🔍 Advanced Commentary — Where the Road Leads
> Honestly: there is no "absolutely correct time" here to calibrate against, only **relative consistency** and **majority-vote fault tolerance**. What the Lobster does is the engineering of Marzullo/Chrony falseticker arbitration — fundamentally a bet that "no more than half fail simultaneously," an assumption that collapses outright when the upstreams share a single stratum-1, or when an entire region's GPS timing is disturbed (all three AZs drift together and the majority vote fails). The real next step is to grade trust in time itself: deploy PTP (hardware timestamps, error down to sub-microsecond) as an **independent second time source** on latency-sensitive critical paths, letting NTP and PTP serve as falseticker detectors for each other; and make "clock trustworthiness" a first-class telemetry — when root dispersion surges, downstream trace analysis should automatically flag "this AZ's timeline is low-trust" rather than swallowing it wholesale. The endgame proposition is: once Agents begin making automated decisions based on telemetry, a trustworthiness score for telemetry's time dimension turns from a nice-to-have into an unbypassable safety precondition.

## Scenario 38 · Hunting Sporadic Errors in the Blind Spot of Sampling Rates
**Background**: To control cost, the tracing system uses head-based sampling at 1%, against a million requests per second.
**Problem**: A 0.03%-probability sporadic 5xx draws endless customer complaints, but at 1% sampling these failed requests are almost never captured — the SRE wants to see one complete failing trace but can't pull one no matter what. Observability has a "structural blind spot" here: the rarer and more important an error, the more easily it gets discarded by sampling.
**Skills Required**: Head- vs tail-based sampling trade-offs, quantifying sampling bias, dynamic sampling strategy design, rare-event capture.
**Open-Source Tools**: OpenTelemetry Collector (tail sampling processor), Grafana Tempo, Jaeger, Prometheus exemplars.
**Agent Capabilities Used**: The Lobster notices from the metrics layer that the 5xx ratio, while low, is persistent, and that the corresponding trace sampling hit rate is near 0 (the textbook signature of a blind spot). A sub-agent dynamically pushes a temporary tail-sampling policy to the OTel Collector: "retain 100% of any trace with status≥500 or latency>1s." Thirty seconds later the first complete failing trace lands; the Lobster posts it into the channel together with the root-cause span, and automatically rescinds the temporary policy after the problem is resolved to keep cost in check.
**Without the Agent**: The SRE temporarily cranks the global sampling rate to 100% (cost and storage instantly explode) while praying to catch one failing trace, then forgets to dial it back after catching it, and the month-end bill triples.
**Payoff**: Capturing a complete trace of a rare error went from "almost impossible" to within 30 seconds; and because the policy auto-rescinds, storage cost grows by zero.

> 💡 A Word to the Wise
> "The cruelty of 1% sampling is that it precisely discards the 0.03% you most need to see — the rarer the catastrophe, the more gently 'averaging' tucks it away."

> 🔍 Advanced Commentary — Where the Road Leads
> This road is "observability that focuses on demand": observability shouldn't be a uniform net, it should be like an immune system — low-cost at rest, converging when an enemy appears. Here the Lobster plays "dynamic adjuster of observation resolution." The engineering tension in landing this is the stability of the control loop — if the Lobster cranks sampling to the max because of one bit of jitter, and that max triggers more jitter, you get observational self-oscillation. What this road needs in the future is a closed loop with damping and a cost budget: solving "I want to see clearly" and "I can't afford this much" within the same objective function.

## Scenario 39 · Multi-Window Early Warning of SLO Burn Rate
**Background**: A core API's monthly error budget is managed by an SLO, with traditional static-threshold alerting.
**Problem**: A canary release introduced a slow memory leak; the error rate never breached any instantaneous threshold (only 0.4% more 5xx per minute), so the alerts stayed silent throughout — but at this rate it would burn through the entire month's error budget in 6 hours. This is the most insidious gray failure: no single-point anomaly anywhere, yet the system is marching at a steady pace toward an SLO violation.
**Skills Required**: Multi-window multi-burn-rate alerting design, error-budget modeling, burn-rate extrapolation, canary-rollback decisions.
**Open-Source Tools**: Prometheus, Sloth, Pyrra, Grafana.
**Agent Capabilities Used**: The Lobster simultaneously computes burn rates for the 1h/6h fast windows and the 3d slow window. A sub-agent finds the 6h-window burn rate steady at 4× and extrapolates "the budget will hit zero in 5h48m"; even though no instantaneous alert has fired, it proactively escalates to an early warning, pushing the burn-curve extrapolation chart, the suspect canary batch, and a one-click rollback command into the channel together — so humans can decide on a rollback 5 hours before the budget burns out.
**Without the Agent**: Every instantaneous alert is quiet, the on-call has no warning whatsoever, until one midnight the SLO board suddenly turns red, the monthly budget burns out overnight, and customers start timing out en masse — only then realizing the canary six hours earlier planted a mine.
**Payoff**: Shifted from "reactive firefighting after the budget burns out" to "proactive warning 5 hours before"; quarterly SLO-violation incidents fell from 3 to 0.

> 💡 A Word to the Wise
> "The most dangerous failure doesn't scream; it just burns a month's worth of fault tolerance to ash at a pace you can't perceive — static thresholds can see the explosion, but not the smoldering."

> 🔍 Advanced Commentary — Where the Road Leads
> This upgrades the Lobster from "anomaly detector" to "trend extrapolator": it cares not about "is it wrong now" but "at this rate will it go wrong, and when." The value of this road is in moving alerting from after the fact to before. The forward-looking trust dilemma is "the cost of prophecy" — a proactive rollback based on extrapolation means acting before the harm has occurred, and once the extrapolation is wrong, it's a needless rollback disturbance. In the future the Lobster needs to attach a "confidence and uncertainty interval" to every extrapolation, and grade rollback recommendations into "remind / stand by / automatic," letting predictive operations find an accountable balance between boldness and restraint.

## Scenario 40 · Cardinality Cost Attribution and Observability Bill Governance
**Background**: An observability platform with a $600K monthly bill covering metrics/logs/traces, shared by over 200 teams, with no one accountable for the total.
**Problem**: The bill rose 40% within three months, the CFO slammed the table, but nobody knew where the money went — observability itself became an "unobservable" black hole: who wrote the most expensive metric, whose logs took up 35% of ingest, which trace tag is most wasteful — all a tangled mess. It can observe everyone else's system, but not its own cost.
**Skills Required**: Telemetry cost-attribution modeling, per-team chargeback, cardinality/ingest cost decomposition, ROI analysis.
**Open-Source Tools**: Grafana Mimir/Loki (per-tenant usage), OpenCost, Prometheus (`*_ingested_samples_total`), Vector.
**Agent Capabilities Used**: Each week the Lobster attributes metric series count, log bytes, and trace span count by tenant/team labels. A sub-agent computes "the top ten by cost + year-over-year growth + each metric's query hit rate (has it ever been referenced by a dashboard/alert)," surfacing a batch of "zombie metrics" that burn $40K a month yet have never been queried by any dashboard. It produces a per-team chargeback report and decommission recommendations locally, and via the Multi-Channel Gateway pushes them to each team's owner and the finance channel simultaneously, attaching proof that "cutting these affects no existing alert."
**Without the Agent**: The platform team manually exports the bill CSV, hand-splits by tenant, and emails 200 teams one by one to reconcile; one round of reporting takes two weeks, and by the time it's done the bill has risen again — forever unable to catch up.
**Payoff**: Cost attribution went from two weeks of manual work to weekly automatic output; the first round of zombie-metric decommissioning saved $110K a month, with zero alerts affected.

> 💡 A Word to the Wise
> "An observability platform that can't observe its own spending is itself the biggest gray failure — you can see the pulse of the entire system, yet can't see that you're bleeding out."

> 🔍 Advanced Commentary — Where the Road Leads
> This road carries the Lobster onto a battlefield engineers often avoid: "the economics of observability." Observation isn't free; every metric, every log line is interest-bearing debt. Here the Lobster plays "financial auditor of telemetry," evaluating cost and value (query hit rate) in tandem. The forward-looking engineering proposition is to make "cost" a first-class property of telemetry, priced at the CI stage alongside cardinality and retention. The ultimate trust boundary remains the decommission decision — a zombie metric not queried today doesn't mean it won't be needed in the next major incident. To let an Agent dare to cut, yet not cut the lifeline, requires reversible governance of "freeze rather than delete" — and that is the optimal solution to cost versus safety.
